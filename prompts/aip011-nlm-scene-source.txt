AIP011 — NotebookLM Scene Source Writer

You are going to write a scene-format source document for a NotebookLM cinematic video. I will tell you the book or topic at the end of this prompt.

The output will be uploaded to NotebookLM as a source document. NotebookLM will use it to generate a cinematic documentary-style video. Your job is to write scenes that give the video engine something physical and cinematic to render — not an essay, not a summary.

---

OUTPUT FORMAT

The document has three parts:

1. TITLE AND OVERVIEW
A short title and 2-4 sentences explaining the topic, the angle, and why it matters.

2. SCENES (6-8 scenes)
Each scene follows this structure exactly:

Scene N — [Short Descriptive Title]

[1-3 paragraphs of narrative prose. One idea. Concrete and visual. Camera intent embedded in the prose — not labeled.]

SETTING: [Physical location and time of day]
SUBJECT: [Who or what is the physical anchor]
IN-SCENE MOTION: [What is moving within the frame]
LIGHTING: [Specific lighting condition]
NARRATION BEAT: [What concept or information is being conveyed in the audio]

3. KEY TAKEAWAY
2 sentences. The central idea the viewer walks away with.

---

SCENE WRITING RULES

Follow these rules for every scene:

RULE 1 — Every scene needs a physical anchor.
Ground each scene in something physical: a person doing something, an environment with inherent motion, an object being used, or an atmospheric condition. Do not write abstract scenes.

Weak: "The concept of resilience permeates the narrative."
Strong: "A woman sits at a workbench under a single lamp, reassembling a broken mechanism with steady hands. The camera pushes in slowly on her face."

RULE 2 — Describe motion explicitly.
State what is moving within the frame. Do not assume the engine will infer it.
Reliable motion: people walking, gesturing, reacting; wind moving fabric; water flowing; fire flickering; crowds moving; simple object interactions.

RULE 3 — Embed camera intent in the prose.
Do not label the camera action. Write it into the scene description.

Instead of: "CAMERA: slow zoom in"
Write: "The camera pushes in slowly on his face as the light flickers."

Reliable camera actions to embed:
- Push-in / slow zoom in: signals focus, intensity. Use for key revelations.
- Pull-back / crane up: signals scale, consequence. Use for establishing scope.
- Tracking shot: follows a subject in motion. Use for progress, journey.
- Aerial / crane shot: establishes geography, epic scale.
- Handheld: adds grit, tension, documentary feel.
- Slow pan: connection, lateral survey. Slow only — fast pans smear.
- Rack focus: shift in attention from foreground to background or vice versa.

Never combine movements in one scene.

RULE 4 — Use specific lighting.
Name the lighting condition in every scene. Examples:
- "Golden hour light raking across stone walls"
- "Flickering candlelight reflecting in the subject's eyes"
- "Cold blue shadow filling the room"
- "Harsh noon light, no shadows"
- "Green glow of an early computer terminal"

RULE 5 — Write as a screenwriter, not a narrator.
The narration delivers the ideas. The scene description governs what is visually happening at the same time. Each scene is a unit of physical action.

RULE 6 — No apostrophes or single quotes in the text.
Rephrase to avoid them. Use em-dashes instead of contractions where needed.

---

STRUCTURE GUIDANCE

- Aim for 6-8 scenes. More shorter scenes is better than fewer longer ones.
- Each scene moves the idea forward — not sideways. Build like a story unfolding.
- The opening scene should establish the core tension or hook.
- The closing scene before the Key Takeaway should resolve or land the central insight.
- Split a scene if it covers more than one distinct idea or shifts setting mid-paragraph.

---

TRANSLATING ABSTRACT IDEAS INTO PHYSICAL SCENES

For books about ideas, psychology, or concepts, convert each idea into a physical situation before writing:

- Where would this idea live in the physical world?
- Who would be present?
- What would they be doing?

Examples:
- "The passage of time" -- timelapse of light moving across a stone floor, camera tilting up slowly
- "An expert who was wrong" -- a confident figure at a lectern, audience nodding, then a later scene: the same figure alone, reading a single page, face changing
- "Collaboration" -- two figures at opposite ends of a table, papers spread between them, slowly moving closer as they work
