Germany's Castles

A record of a thousand years of political fragmentation

The Hook

Germany has more castles than the United States has McDonald's. While the US has roughly 14,000 locations, Germany boasts between 20,000 and 25,000 castles—intact structures, romantic ruins, and documented foundations. This averages to one every 25 kilometers across a country the size of Montana.

Why So Many?

  • Imperial Patchwork Medieval Germany was the Holy Roman Empire—a patchwork of hundreds of independent territories. Rulers traveled constantly, building castles every 15 miles to house their courts during transit.
  • Status Symbols Every count, duke, and bishop needed a fortified residence as a "business address" and seat of government.
  • Political Fragmentation Germany remained fragmented far longer than centralizing powers like France, leading to centuries of competitive building.

The Raubritter Era

The "robber knights" were minor nobles who turned hilltop fortresses into extortion tools during the Great Interregnum (1250–1273).

"The most brazen stretched iron chains across the river from their castle banks, physically stopping ships until a fee was paid."

This era is the direct origin of the American English phrase "robber baron."

Burg Eltz

Moselle Valley • 12th Century

Rising from a narrow rock in a forested gorge, Eltz has been owned by the same family for nearly 900 years. Its organic, "lopsided" appearance results from three family branches building separate wings around a single courtyard.

Status: Privately owned, open Apr-Nov

Neuschwanstein

Bavaria • 1869–1886

A 19th-century Wagnerian fantasy commissioned by King Ludwig II. Not a fortress, but a stage set for mythology. It inspired Disney's Sleeping Beauty Castle and the studio's iconic logo.

Status: 1.3 million annual visitors

Wartburg Castle

Thuringia • Founded 1067

Where Martin Luther lived as "Junker Jörg" in 1521. Here he translated the New Testament into German, effectively standardizing the modern German language.

Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site

Heidelberg Castle

Baden-Württemberg • 14th–17th Century

The "most famous ruin in Germany." Its pink sandstone walls loom over the old town. Home to the world's largest wine barrel (219,000 liters).

Status: Iconic Romantic Ruin

Hohenzollern Castle

Swabian Alps • 1846–1867

Ancestral home of Prussian kings and German Emperors. Rebuilt three times, the current neo-Gothic structure houses the Prussian royal crown.

Status: Private property of heirs

Marksburg Castle

Rhine Gorge • 12th Century

The only Rhine hilltop castle that was never destroyed. It remains the most complete medieval preservation on the river, including a torture chamber and knights' hall.

Status: Never captured or destroyed

Schwerin Castle

Mecklenburg-Vorpommern • 1845–1857

A fairy-tale island castle reflected in lake water. Modeled after the French Château de Chambord, it currently houses the state parliament.

Status: Active Legislature House

Frankenstein Castle

Hesse • 13th Century

Ruins named after the "Franckenstein" lords. Linked to alchemist Johann Konrad Dippel, who allegedly tried to animate dead tissue with "animal oil."

Status: Now a Halloween destination

The Rhine Gorge

Bingen to Koblenz

The most castle-dense stretch of river in the world. Over 40 castle sites in 65 kilometers. Highlights include Pfalzgrafenstein (built in the middle of the river) and the massive Rheinfels ruin.

The Moselle Valley

Steep Vineyard Terraces

Winding through steep ridges, the Moselle features Eltz, Cochem, and Thurant. Often cited as the more beautiful and less crowded alternative to the Rhine.

Bavaria

Royal Alpine Splendor

Concentration of Wittelsbach palaces and Ludwig II's fantasy constructions, centered around the Alps and pristine lakes.

Central Germany

Thuringia & Saxony

Home to older Romanesque structures like Wartburg and the Kyffhäuser ruins, steeped in imperial mythology and the legend of the sleeping Emperor Barbarossa.

Primary Functions

  • Defense: Thick walls and hilltop visibility controlled territory.
  • Residence: Noble social centers and seats of justice.
  • Administration: Local government where taxes and rents were collected.
  • Revenue: Toll booths on major river arteries.
  • Status: Visible demonstrations of rank and political power through height.

The Decline & Rebirth

Castles became obsolete with gunpowder and the rise of city courts. Many were destroyed by Louis XIV's armies or the Thirty Years' War.

The 19th-century Romantic Movement saved them. Ruins were no longer eyesores but "sublime objects." This led to the restoration of Rhine castles and the "neo-medieval" building boom.

Burg

Fortified medieval castle for defense and residence.

Schloss

Palace or manor house; functional rather than defensive.

Festung

Large-scale fortress designed purely for military use.

Bergfried

Tall, narrow central keep used as a final refuge.

Raubritter

"Robber knight" using a castle for extortion/banditry.

Ganerben

Joint heirs co-owning a castle in separate wings.
