Medieval Animal Trials

A Peculiar Jurisprudence

Between the years 900 and 1750 CE, European legal systems engaged in a practice that strikes the modern mind as surreal: the formal criminal prosecution of animals. These were not mere culls or impulsive acts of vengeance, but sophisticated legal proceedings complete with judges, defense attorneys, and sworn testimony.

The practice was rooted in a complex theological framework where all of creation was seen as subject to divine law. If an animal violated the natural order—particularly through homicide or crop destruction—it was believed that justice must be restored through formal ritual and legal judgment.

Secular Criminal Trials

Handled domestic animals (pigs, cows, horses) accused of physical harm to humans. These trials often resulted in capital punishment carried out by professional executioners.

Ecclesiastical Proceedings

Focused on "vermin" (rats, weevils, locusts) that damaged crops. Since they couldn't be caught individually, the Church issued formal warnings, excommunications, or curses.

France, 1386

The Sow of Falaise

A pig was accused of murdering an infant. In a bizarre display of legal theater, the sow was dressed in human clothes—a jacket and breeches—before being publicly hanged in the town square.

Verdict: Guilty, Execution
Switzerland, 1474

The Rooster of Basel

A rooster was brought to trial for the "heinous and unnatural crime" of laying an egg. In an era where this was seen as a portent of sorcery or the birth of a cockatrice, the bird was burned at the stake.

Verdict: Sorcery, Stake
Savigny, 1457

The Sow & Six Piglets

A sow and her six piglets were charged with killing a five-year-old. While the sow was condemned, the piglets were acquitted on account of their youth and the "bad example" of their mother.

Verdict: Partial Acquittal
France, c. 1519

The Rats of Autun

Accused of destroying barley crops, the rats were defended by Bartholomew Chassenée, who famously argued they couldn't attend court because the journey was too dangerous due to cats.

Outcome: Legal Delay
Savoy, 1587

The Weevils' Compromise

After years of litigation regarding vineyard destruction, the villagers of St. Julien offered the weevils a specific plot of fertile land in exchange for leaving the vineyards alone.

Outcome: Land Settlement
Aosta, 1584

The Caterpillars' Curse

When caterpillars decimated the local greenery, the ecclesiastical court followed strict procedure, eventually issuing a formal decree of excommunication against the insects.

Sentence: Excommunication
Moussy, 1314

The Bull of Moussy

Charged with homicide after goring a man. The court treated the animal exactly as a human killer, ordering it to be hanged from the village gallows.

Verdict: Hanged
Dijon, 1314

The Horse of Dijon

A horse found guilty of homicide. Condemned to death by the local magistrate to satisfy the requirement of blood for blood as per current custom.

Verdict: Death
Senlis, 1567

The Pig of Senlis

Following the killing of a child, this pig's execution was so high-profile it led to new local regulations regarding animal husbandry and legal liability.

Outcome: Execution & Policy
⚖️

Bartholomew Chassenée

Jurist & Defender of the Rats (1480–1541)

A brilliant French jurist whose career was launched by his ingenious defense of the rats of Autun. He argued that since the summons had been read in every parish, the rats were aware of the trial, but were prevented from attending by the "mortal enemies" (cats) lying in wait on all the roads.

He later became the First President of the Parlement of Provence. His masterpiece, Consilium primum, remains the most detailed historical treatise on how and why animals should be treated as legal subjects.

Theological & Legal Foundation

The medieval worldview did not see nature as an "other" but as a subset of God's kingdom. Following the Fall of Adam, creation was seen as corrupted but still subject to the Great Chain of Being. Animals, though lacking reason, were believed to be capable of knowing they had violated the peace of the community.

  • Recognition of animal agency within a divine hierarchy.
  • Requirement for "Blood Atonement" in cases of homicide.
  • The belief that formal legal rituals could restore cosmic order.

The Secular Route

Applied to domestic animals. Procedure: Arrest, Imprisonment (often in human jails), Trial, Sentence (hanging, burying alive, or burning).

The Church Route

Applied to pests. Procedure: Petition to the Bishop, Three Warnings (monitory), Exorcism or Excommunication if they fail to depart.

"The practice declined through the Enlightenment as philosophy drew sharper distinctions between human reason and animal instinct."
